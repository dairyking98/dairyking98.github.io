A 1909 Blickensderfer No. 5 on Blickensderfer's own **DHIATENSOR** keyboard
rather than QWERTY. It is a genuinely different machine rather than a variation
on the standard theme, and it has fewer parts than almost anything else of its
period.

{% include machine-spec.html %}

## No typebars

Instead of a basket of typebars, the Blickensderfer carries every character on a
single cylindrical **typewheel**, which rotates and tilts to present the right
one to the platen. It's the same principle IBM would reach for six decades later
with the Selectric's typeball, arrived at in the 1890s.

It doesn't use a ribbon either — it inks from a roller. Between that and the
typewheel, the whole machine has far fewer moving parts than a contemporary
typebar portable, which is most of why these were light enough to be genuinely
portable when nothing else was.

## The DHIATENSOR keyboard

The keyboard is the other departure. DHIATENSOR puts the most frequently used
letters on the bottom row, on the argument that this is where the fingers rest —
a rival efficiency claim to QWERTY, made decades before Dvorak.

## The typewheel

A worn or cracked typewheel is the usual reason a Blickensderfer sits unused.
They're consumable parts, machine-specific, and no longer made — which is what
made a printable replacement worth designing. The
[type element project]({{ '/projects/type-elements/' | relative_url }}) includes
a parametric OpenSCAD typewheel, printed in resin, dimensioned from this machine.
