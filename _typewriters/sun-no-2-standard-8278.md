A cheap machine, built to be cheap, and much more interesting for it. The Sun
Typewriter Company sold the No. 2 at a price the big standards couldn't touch —
the original advertising I have puts it at **$40 in 1901** — and nearly every
design decision in it follows from that constraint. It is, as far as I know, the
first typewriter built with sheet metal in place of an all-cast frame, and the
mechanism is stripped down to match.

This one came to me broken in the way Suns usually break, and getting it typing
again took a machine shop, a packet of obsolete screws, and a new ink roller.

## The broken shift casting

The machine arrived with a fractured casting: the forked section that guides a
shaft up and down to shift the platen between figures and capitals. It's a known
weak point on these — a thin fork carrying a working load, in a casting chosen
because it was cheap to produce.

I stripped the machine down and isolated the casting, then took it to **Gonsel's
Machine Shop in Oakland**. They machined the fracture faces down flat, drilled
and tapped, and attached stock material to rebuild the fork. It's a repair rather
than a replacement — there is no supply of new Sun castings — and the machine
shifts properly again.

## Screws you cannot buy

Several screws on the machine were bent. Replacing them turned out to be the
fiddliest part of the job, because the Sun uses a **6-36 thread** — a pitch that
has long since fallen out of use.

I found new old stock on eBay and bought a large packet to use two of them.
They happened to match the originals in head shape as well as thread: domed head,
slotted drive. For a machine where correctness of small parts is most of what
separates a restoration from a repair, that was luck rather than planning.

## The inking system

The Sun doesn't use a ribbon. It inks from rollers, and the arrangement is
unusual even by the standards of ribbonless machines: a **small ink roller that
does the work, and a larger reserve roller feeding it**. In operation the small
roller bounces up against the large one, taking a fresh charge of ink each time.

The original assembly was riveted in place, which is the cheap-to-produce
decision showing up again — riveted parts aren't meant to be serviced. I drilled
out the riveted shaft, removed the perished original roller, and fitted a new
**IR40-type ink roller**, retained with a small threaded screw and nut instead of
a rivet. That makes it serviceable next time, which the factory never intended.

The machine is fully functional now.

## The escapement

The other thing worth pointing at is how the escapement is triggered. A
conventional typewriter uses a universal bar — a single bar that every typebar
strikes on its way to the platen, which then trips the escapement once per
keystroke. The Sun does without it: **each typebar trips the escapement
directly**. Fewer parts, less to go wrong, and cheaper to build. It's the same
logic as the sheet metal frame, applied to the mechanism.

## Base and cover

The machine is mounted on its wooden base and has its original metal cover.
Either survives on its own often enough; having **both, together, with the
machine they came with** is uncommon — covers got separated, bases got broken up,
and neither was worth keeping once the machine stopped being used.

## Why this one

Sixth in my own ranking of the collection, for its age and its uniqueness. It's
the oldest machine here that I've had substantially rebuilt rather than merely
cleaned, and the design is genuinely odd rather than just early: no ribbon, no
universal bar, sheet metal where everything else of the period is castings.
