## The uncommon part: a QWERTY keyboard

The obvious oddity here is the keyboard. Most Model 1s came with Hammond's own
**Ideal** layout — a curved two-row keyboard arranged to Hammond's argument about
letter frequency — and were sold installed in a decorative wooden cabinet. This
one is **QWERTY**, on the wood base and top rather than a cabinet.

That combination is what made it worth buying. An Ideal-keyboard Model 1 in a
cabinet is the configuration you expect to find; this isn't.

## The split shuttle

The Hammond doesn't use typebars. Its type sits on a **shuttle** — a curved
element carrying the whole character set, which swings into position so the
hammer can strike the paper against it from behind. The Model 1 uses a *split*
shuttle, in two pieces rather than the single-piece shuttles of later Hammonds
like the Multiplex.

It is the most delicate part of the machine by a wide margin: intricate,
precise, and carrying fine type detail on a thin section. It is also,
predictably, the part that is most often broken. A Model 1 with an undamaged
original split shuttle is meaningfully rarer than a Model 1.

The originals appear to be **vulcanised rubber**, which explains both the crispness
of the type and the failure mode — vulcanised rubber embrittles with age, so a
shuttle that has survived 135 years intact has been lucky as much as careful.

How the shuttles were actually made is described in
**[*Scientific American*, Vol. 74, No. 10, 7 March 1896](https://www.jstor.org/stable/e26116597)**,
which is close to contemporary with this machine — six years after it left the
factory, while the Model 1 was still in production.

## Reference machine for the printed shuttles

This machine is the one I designed and iterated the
[3D-printed Hammond Model 1 type elements]({{ '/projects/type-elements/' | relative_url }})
against. Having an intact original to measure, fit, and compare against is the
difference between a part that drops in and a part that nearly does — and since
broken shuttles are the normal condition for these machines, an undamaged
reference is worth more than a spare.

The printed replacements are the practical answer to a part that can't be bought,
can't easily be repaired once cracked, and was made from a material that was
never going to last indefinitely.
