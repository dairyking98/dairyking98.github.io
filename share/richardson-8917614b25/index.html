Model 1 selection: Richardson's opposed plates on bead chain (I-060)¶

The current direction for Model 1 selection (2026-11-09), replacing the two bails and shuttle. It starts from James Richardson's US554730 (1896), and swaps his rods, gear sector and pinion for one closed bead-chain loop.

Status: first layout. Every dimension, mass and force here is a hand estimate, with no CAD or test behind it yet. More views: 3D model, wheel travel, plate shapes, tolerance stack. Motion after the pinch: lock pin, hammer trip and dwell options.

What Richardson did¶

From the patent text and Figs. 2 to 7:

  • The key levers are free bell-cranks, "without positive connection with any other part" except their pivot. Pressing one swings its lower arm against two plates.
  • Two rectangular frames hang from a shaft and a sleeve around it. Each carries a plate, and the plates are stacked one just above the other (Fig. 5). Each frame is one bent U of sheet (Fig. 7).
  • The plate edges are stepped along oblique lines (Fig. 6). Each key has its own pair of steps, and the plates are interchangeable.
  • A rod from each frame pulls one arm of a gear sector, so one frame goes forward as the other comes back. The sector turns a long pinion on the upright type shaft.
  • The key pushes whichever plate is nearer until the other arrives. "Its motion is stopped by the opposing action of the plates." The patent's rule is that the sum of the movements equals the frames' throw less the amount cut from the edges.
  • There is no home position. The wheel goes "forward or back to any position from any other", and "releasing the key has no effect". In his worked example one word takes 5 spaces of wheel travel, against 104 for a rotating wheel and 126 for one that returns home each time.
  • The pinched key locks the wheel in both directions for as long as it stays down.

What changes¶

1896 Model 1 Why
Two rods, a gear sector and a long pinion One closed bead-chain loop. Frame 1 clamps to one run and frame 2 to the other The two runs of a loop already move in opposite directions, so the chain is both the opposing link and the drive. Two wraps instead of six, and no gear backlash
Pull rods Short drag link from each frame arm to its chain clamp Keeps the clamp moving along the run as the arm swings (R-014)
One lever pivot, so key dip differs by row Three pivot rods stacked under the contact line, with a riser length for each row Every row gets the same 18 mm dip and the same tip travel
Cast or filed parts Laser-cut levers and plates from generated DXF, with both plate edges cut in one setup from the same datum Each plate's edges locate from its own holes, and new layouts are a new file
Bare lever edge on bare plate Small replaceable pad on each lever tip Softens the pick-up impact. Both plates compress it equally, so it doesn't move the letter
The pinch is the only lock Pinch sets the position, and the tapered pin still takes the strike The strike load must never reach the chain (D-0001)
Plain bearings Ball bearings at the frames, levers, chain wheels and element About 45 in all

Laser tolerance: position depends on half the difference between a key's two edges, so ±0.08 mm on each edge gives at most ±0.08 mm of plate movement, or ±0.2 column. The tapered pin can correct ±0.4. Frame pivots, drag links and chain pitch add to that, and the chain needs measuring.

What it costs the finger¶

Mass felt at the keytop, estimated with the thin cup element:

Item Mass at keytop
Element about 219 g
Two frames with plates and chain arms about 36 g
Chain and clamps (they move 3× the plate speed) about 31 g
Lever and keytop about 7 g
Total, on every move about 290 g

That's more than the 235 g in the report, because the report didn't include the chain and step-up arm. With a 25 g solid element it would be about 950 g, so the cup element is essential to this design.

The mass is the same near or far, but most moves are short because there's no return home. Grouping common letters together on the wheel, as Richardson suggests, shortens them further.

Pick-up impact is the main concern. At a 0.25 m/s keystroke the tip picks up a stationary load of about 0.55 kg effective mass. With a hard tip (3 ms contact) that's about 24 N at the keytop, and with a soft pad (8 ms) about 9 N. A similar impact happens when the second plate arrives, about 9 mJ each.

How the forces go¶

  • Selecting: the tip pushes one plate, and the frame arm pulls one chain run. The loop drives the spline and draws the other frame back.
  • At the pinch: the tip presses both plates the same way, so both runs pull equally and the element gets no torque. Chain stretch doesn't move the letter, and the load goes into the spline and idler bearings.
  • Bottom of the stroke: a hard stop on the universal bail, just past the pinch, keeps a heavy finger from loading the chain.
  • Printing: the tapered pin locks the element, so the strike stays out of the chain.

Open¶

  • Plate sag and twist: the chain tie is at the centre and the tips reach ±106 mm. The plate is stiff in its own plane, but this needs checking in CAD.
  • Plate 1 needs a slot for plate 2's chain arm to pass through, about 6 × 30 mm near the centre.
  • Clearance cut-outs: home- and bottom-row risers pass the other rods, so each needs a slot about 8 mm wide and a riser about 14 mm wide.
  • Universal bail: the rows swing through different angles, so each lug needs its own radius (about 25, 32 and 39 mm).
  • Pad or lead-in: I've drawn a soft pad, which keeps the plate edges free of ramps. A short lead-in on the plate edges would be the alternative.
  • Rollover: a second key pressed while the first is down hits plates that are already pinched. Richardson's pawl lets the next key start early, and I haven't worked that out here.
  • Lever pivots: bearings add about 28 parts for roughly 1% less force. A plain rod with spacers is a fair first build.

Test first¶

  1. Pinch rig: one lever, two plates, a dummy inertia of about 0.55 kg effective at the tip. Measure rebound, noise and force with hard and soft tips. Pass: rebound under ±0.2 column.
  2. Chain loop: two clamps, one spline, an idler in a slot. Measure backlash at the element with both runs loaded, and with one.
  3. Edge accuracy: measure step positions on a laser-cut sample plate from the shop you'd use.
