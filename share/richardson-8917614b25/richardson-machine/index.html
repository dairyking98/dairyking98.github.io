How Richardson's 1896 machine works¶

A plain reading of James Richardson's US554730, filed 1893 and issued 18 February 1896. The patent's text is an imperfect scan, so a few details may need checking against the drawings.

Everything runs from one key stroke. Each key is a free lever that touches four separate swinging members, and each member does one job.

Member What it's moved by Job
Two selection plates, on opposed frames Each key, by its own pair of steps Turn the type wheel to the letter, then lock it
Press frame Every key, by the full stroke Ink the type, and store energy for the print
Spacing plate (the "third bar") Every key, by its own step Feed the paper by that letter's width

Key levers¶

All the key levers are the same in function: bell-cranks on one common rod, separated by washers, passing through a comb with a spring under each. In front of the comb they fan out to the keytops (Fig. 1). Below the rod, their working edges are vertical and "all in the same plane". What makes each key different is cut into the plates, not the lever.

Selection¶

Two rectangular frames hang from a shaft and a sleeve around it. Each carries a plate with a stepped edge, one just above the other. A rod from each frame pulls an arm of a gear sector, so one frame goes forward as the other comes back. The sector turns a long pinion on the upright shaft carrying the type shell.

A key pushes whichever plate is nearer until the other arrives and pinches it. The sum of the two movements equals the full throw less what's cut from the edges, so the position is the same wherever the plates started. There is no home position. Releasing the key changes nothing, and the wheel stays put until the next key.

His worked example: one word takes 5 spaces of wheel travel, against 104 for a rotating wheel and 126 for one returning home each time.

Inking¶

Richardson uses an ink pad, with no ribbon.

  1. As the key goes down, the press frame draws a rod that lifts the pad's bar through a pawl and arm.
  2. The pad rises in front of the type, and a bend at the top of its slot presses it against the face.
  3. A small inking roller keeps the pad supplied, and a ratchet turns it a little each stroke.

Impression¶

The wheel stays still and the paper strikes it, driven by a spring the finger has already wound.

  1. Lifting the pad stretches a spring. Near the end of the rise, a screw trips the pawl and the spring snaps the pad back down.
  2. On the way down, a pin on the pad's bar hits a pawl on the rock shaft under the paper carriage. That pawl swings aside on the way up but acts as a cam on the way down, rocking the shaft and throwing the paper into the type.
  3. A second spring rocks the shaft back.
  4. A solid surface inside the type shell, with a small anti-friction roller, takes the blow.

The print force doesn't depend on how hard the key is pressed, and the pinched key holds the wheel locked throughout.

Escapement and the third bar¶

There's no escapement wheel and no mainspring. A pawl engages a rack on the back of the paper carriage. Pressing a key draws the pawl back. Releasing it lets a spring pull the pawl forward by that same distance, pushing the carriage along. A stop pin clamps the pawl to the rack so the carriage can't overrun.

The distance comes from the third bar, the spacing plate. Its edge is mostly straight, with a few changes:

  • Projections for m and w, which swing it furthest, for the widest feed.
  • Notches for f, i, j, l and t, for the narrowest.
  • Straight edge for the rest, such as n, o and s.

So it's a proportionally spaced machine, using ordinary printer's type at full width. A turnbuckle adjusts the overall spacing. The space bar acts only on this frame, and pressing it partway makes a shorter space, for justifying lines. A separate key lifts the feed pawl so the carriage can be moved freely.

Shift and capitals¶

The type shell has three rows: lower case at the top, capitals at the bottom, and figures and punctuation in the middle. Two thumb keys lift the shaft by a fork until the wanted row is at the print line, and a sliding bar can lock them down.

Capitals are wider, so the capital key also moves a pin along an inclined slot in the feed linkage, which increases the feed. It's a single extra amount for all capitals, not one per letter, and the patent describes it only for capitals.

What we'd use¶

  • The third bar's principle: one bar every key pushes, with an edge that gives each key its own motion. Our universal bail can be built this way, laser-cut, to even out the three rows' different swings and set each key's trip point.
  • Stored-energy print, fired by a trip, so print force doesn't depend on the finger.
  • Feed on the key's return, for both the carriage and the ribbon.
  • Ribbon instead of the pad. Fixed pitch for now, though the proportional spacing is worth a future look.
