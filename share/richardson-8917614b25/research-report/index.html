Roll one bar under notched levers¶

The best clean-sheet Model 1 selector found in the library is a single free-rolling bar on a swinging frame, driven by a unique closed slot cut into each laser-cut key lever, then a gear sector to a long pinion on a thin cup element. Hammond disclosed the rolling bar in 1880 (US224183) and Schuckers the slot with a straight locking extension (US529810); I found no patent combining them with ball-bearing pivots throughout. It removes the idle-side coupling problem outright, because both halves of the keyboard drive one member, and it needs no chain, no linear rail and no spring in the positioning path. My first estimate of the mass seen at the keytop is about 127 g on the worst key and about 21 g frequency-weighted with a cup element. The same sum is about 420 g with the project's 25 g element estimate, so the element matters more than the bearings. A pivot-only tree of five rocking code bails ranks second, and Richardson's opposed swinging plates third. Stored-energy selection gives the lightest touch of all but stays unproposed, because the owner rejected it. This matters because the library's anti-friction parts are sparse and no patent balls every joint: bearings alone cannot deliver the brief, and every force and mass figure below is my own estimate, since the library contains no measured key forces for the machines in question. The owner's recorded 15 to 20 mm stroke and the brief's "short strokes" genuinely conflict; I sized everything at 18 mm as the recorded decision and show below what a shorter stroke costs, leaving the choice to the owner.

Inertia, not bearing friction, sets the keytop load¶

Two figures that motivate this clean sheet need a plain label. The abandoned design's 304 g shuttle mass and its six bead-chain wraps are calculations made in this research session from the generator's numbers; neither figure is recorded in the repository under those words. Only the 4× motion ratio could be confirmed from the published constants: 2:1 tackle, 0.61 mm of bail and 2.443 mm of shuttle per column (selection.md). Reflected mass goes with the square of the ratio, so 304 g at 4× is about 4.9 kg at the bail, before the key-to-bail ratio. That number is a session calculation too, and it is not like-for-like with the keytop figures below.

The library holds no measured key force, dip or typing speed for Blickensderfer, Hammond, Commercial Visible or Bennett, and the Selectric appears by name only. The real measurements are Hildebrand's typebar figures: finger or key speed of 40 to 50 cm/s and 30 to 43 ms from key start to impact (Hildebrand 1954, 1955). Single-element evidence is qualitative. Reinecke calls keyboard type-wheel machines unsatisfactory in speed and impression force because of "the large mass of the type wheel to be moved" and the long key travel (Reinecke 1953). No patent gives a before-and-after key force for any anti-friction part. Project keytop budgets of 6.0 and 11.8 N against a limit of 8 N are estimates for a different machine (comb-no1/mechanism.md).

Inertia is what the inventors themselves worried about. Blickensderfer wrote that "the lighter the wheel the greater the ease with which its inertia will be overcome" (US432296). Gardner gave the design rule that a sleeve "avoids the momentum of the segment" but "involves a greater angular motion" (US463860). What the finger feels is J·(dθ/dx)². On a full 28-column wheel the worst key must turn the element 173.6° in one direction, so the only free variables are element inertia J and the selecting length of the stroke.

All numbers in this table are my estimates, not sources. A cup element here means a 1.2 mm resin wall on Ø36 mm with type, perforated web, aluminium pinion and steel shaft: about 23 g in total and J about 1.4×10⁻⁶ kg·m². The "25 g solid" column takes the project's element estimate (I-039) with an assumed 15 mm radius of gyration, J about 5.6×10⁻⁶ kg·m². Force assumes a constant key speed of 0.25 m/s and 20% blends at each end of the motion.

Here is the conflict the brief leaves open. The owner recorded "15-20 mm", 18 mm at every key, trip at about 17.7 mm (I-046 revised); the brief asks for short strokes. A key-driven selector cannot have both at low force: reflected mass scales with 1/S² and blend force with 1/S³, so 8 mm costs five times the 18 mm mass and twelve times the force. At a measured-typebar finger speed of 0.5 m/s the 18 mm cup figure quadruples to about 16 N, so the far columns will not be taken at full speed, as the period machines' reputation already suggests. DE271253 got 2 to 3 mm keys only "through the automatic setting ... by means of the driving force of the paper cart" (DE271253), which is stored energy. Short strokes therefore require either reopening stored energy or changing the element layout (see the conclusion). I did not choose between them; the candidates are sized at 18 mm because that is the recorded decision. The 15 and 20 mm rows are the sensitivity.

Grading softens this. Reflected mass in a graded design goes with column offset squared, so the mean across 28 keys is 36% of the worst key, and about 11% if frequent letters sit nearest home, a period practice (US253475, US463464). The 11% uses standard English letter frequencies, which are my assumption and not in the library.

Three findings reshape the choice. I found no single-element patent with ball bearings at every joint of the selection train: balls appear at element axes and shuttles, single rollers at one contact, and caged balls on selector slides only in DE234683. Second, a one-way contact between key and shared actuator is unavoidable in every layout, so the realistic goal is a contact that rolls or barely moves, not its removal. Third, the patents accept a loose drive and correct it late. IBM states it outright: the detent "insures accuracy while permitting looseness in the drive system" (US2895584). That is what makes a light, slightly loose, all-bearing train viable at all.

Two corrections to the project's own record bear on family 6 and 8. DE271253, cited beside a whiffletree in I-040, is a series lost-motion stack of tube sections; the nearest whiffletree precedent is DE745913, then Palmer. And the Wagner patents read are key-driven (US393318), not the spring-driven family I-052 assumes.

Three candidates can go into Creo now, and the rolling bar leads¶

All three share an output and a lock, described once. A sheet gear sector drives an elongated pinion, as in Richardson (US554730), so the ±5 mm row shift never disengages the drive. The cup element runs on two 688ZZ on a shift carrier hung from parallel links, borrowing the "jointed rectangle" that Lambert used to support a key (US607270), so shift needs no slide. An interior fixed backing carries the blow, after Richardson and Arnold (US448996, US410629); the cup never touches it during selection. A universal bail on 688ZZ bearings drives a tapered pin into one of 28 countersinks, on the carrier so it seats in every row, as already planned in I-029. Gardner, McCool and Bennett all lock late like this (US537606, US964045, US810024). Strike load goes to the lock, never into the train.

Unless stated, every dimension and mass below is a first estimate of mine, unverified by CAD or test. Levers follow the project's working values: 70 mm keytop to pivot, 18 mm stroke equal to 14.7°, 2 mm sheet, 7.33 mm pitch (selection.md).

Rank 1, candidate A: one rolling bar, a slot in every lever¶

Layout. Twenty-eight levers lie side by side. A hardened bar runs across their tails on a three-hanger frame that swings on one rock shaft, like Hammond's: "The bar e is preferably cylindrical in form, is pivoted, and rolls to save sliding friction", fixed in the arms of a "vibratory frame" (US224183). At rest every tail sits below the bar's plane, the clearance idea of I-046. A pressed tail rises, takes the bar into its own closed slot, and the slot's inclination carries the bar fore or aft. A straight final extension parallel to the tail's path then holds the bar still while the key finishes, Schuckers's "straight extension" in which the frame is "stationary and locked" (US529810). The frame carries the sector.

That is about 85 bearings, all off the shelf, in line with R-013. All 28 levers are unique, which the owner has accepted: "I can use unique cams on all parts" (I-037), "the keylever is the pin and the cam, ideally laser cut metal or flat stock" (I-047).

What still slides, and why. Gear flanks slide inside the mesh, because a teeth-free drive would have meant a tensioned band; the late lock tolerates the backlash. The lock pin wedges into its countersink over a fraction of a pitch, and that wedging is the job. The pinion moves axially in the sector during a shift only, and without selection load. The slot itself should roll, but this is the assumption everything depends on: near-home flanks at 2 to 14° are exactly where the project's analysis finds a sliding pin self-locks (below about 14°). They are only usable because the bar turns.

Mass (estimates, cup element). Element 97 g, bar 6 g plus about 5 g for its spin, frame 2 g, sector 9 g, lever and keytop about 8 g. Worst key about 127 g; uniform mean about 51 g; frequency-weighted about 21 g; column 0.5 about 8 g. With the 25 g solid element the worst key is about 420 g. The blends cost peak slope but remove the impact of a sudden start, the idea behind Twiss's slow start (US408150) and Blickensderfer's variable leverage (US459094).

Both halves and home. Slots lean forward for one half, rearward for the other, as Hammond's opposed notches and Schuckers's opposite inclinations do (US423576). There is one actuator, so no idle side and no interlock. Return is positive: the closed slot draws the bar home as the key rises, the arrangement of I-048. A light roller detent in a shallow V, in the spirit of US734044 and US505547, holds the bar at home between strokes, about 0.5 N and not in the print path. Overbanking is impossible by construction, because the bar is captured at every depth and the dwell makes the bottom stop irrelevant to position.

Registration. The slot sets the coarse position, with a stack of about 0.1 column of gear backlash, about 0.15 column of slot profile at ±0.08 mm, and pivots. The tapered pin then corrects within ±0.4 column. The slot's dwell holds position until the pin is in.

Test first. Build one lever, one bar segment and one hanger with a force gauge, and run the shallowest flank (2°) and steepest flank (51°) at 1 to 10 N. Does the bar actually roll on a laser-cut edge, or skid? Then a 10⁵-cycle run for edge damage and recast flaking on stainless, then the cup under a real hammer blow against the fixed backing. Rollover is the known weak point, since a second key's slot mouth can only enter if the bar is back at home. Reinecke records that wheel machines cannot overlap strokes in any case.

Rank 2, candidate B: five rocking code bails and a pivot-only tree¶

Layout. Each lever has laser-cut lugs under the bails of its code, the coded-frame idea of DE271253 and Palmer's interposer projections (DE271253, US2919002). Five bails rock on bearings; Palmer used pivoted crank rods rather than sliding bars. Their outputs sum in a rigid tree of floating levers (DE745913, Palmer) to one output crank, then the shared sector. Weights are +1, +2, +4, +8 and −16, Carmona's signed top bit (US661849), so the rest state is one printing column and the longest move is 14 columns rather than the 27 that I-040 worried about. Palmer's complement link is the precedent for reversing a tree branch.

What still slides. Each lug slips slightly on its bail through arc mismatch; a roller on the bail removes it. Stops are impacts. Palmer's floating links slide in vertical slots, and my layout replaces those with swing links or stops the lever lifts away from, an inference that no patent confirms. Gear and lock as in A.

Mass (estimates). Element about 104 g for a 14-column move, up to about 25 g for active bails and tree, 8 g for the lever: worst key about 137 g, frequency-weighted about 30 g. Binary coding does not reduce the element term; it only makes bail stroke uniform.

Both halves and home. The sign bit makes the two directions; resting lugs never drive idle bails, because a pressed bail swings away from all of them, which disposes of the I-036 problem without a plane trick. Bails return against rest stops on light springs. Springs do not position anything, since each bail is held by a hard stop at print, but a chosen character's force varies with how many bits it has, and the owner asked that key force not vary across the keyboard (I-039).

Registration. As in A, and more dependent on it: the patents correct trees with detents and hard-stopped bits (DE701607, DE745913).

Test first. Model the tree in Creo dynamics and measure the reaction on non-selected bails during element deceleration. By my statics, the idle input is held on its stop during acceleration but is lifted off during deceleration, resisted only by its spring; nothing in the library addresses this. If the required preload exceeds a few tenths of a newton per bail, B loses its key-force argument. A three-bit bench tree in laser-cut sheet then measures real accumulated play.

Rank 3, candidate C: Richardson's opposed plates, positive and homeless¶

Layout. Two frames swing on a shaft and a concentric sleeve, each with an interchangeable graduated edge plate. Both are linked to one sector, so one goes forward as the other comes back. A key tip meets both plates, and "the sum of the movements caused by any key is equal to the extreme throw of the frames, diminished by the amount cut from the edges", after which the key is "stopped by the opposing action of the plates" (US554730). The element goes "forward or back to any position from any other", with no home at all. Richardson's worked example is 5 spaces for a word against 104 or 126 for rotating and oscillating wheels.

What still slides. The tip rises about 0.5 mm along the plate while pushing, the "rubs up the wall" complaint of the I-055 review; the 683 roller handles it. There is no ramp at all, which is its strongest answer to the owner's objections. Gear and lock as in A.

Mass (estimates). Because the whole 347° range must fit in about 16 mm of stroke, element reflected mass is about 200 g on every move, about 235 g with frames, sector and lever, or about 800 g with a 25 g solid element. Shortest-path motion helps time and distance but not ratio. First contact is also an impact: at 0.25 m/s and an assumed 3 ms contact, peak force is about 20 N, far above the blended drive of A.

Both halves and home. The plate edges encode direction, so halves need no separate treatment, and there is no return. Resting keys stay clear only because of the free travel. That is why tips cannot use I-046's plane clearance here: a no-home plate can sit anywhere when a key rises, so the plane trick would need a home return and would give up the patent's main advantage.

Registration. The pinch positions the sector without any spring or backlash upstream of it (my reading, not Richardson's words); the late lock still applies at the element.

Test first. A single-lever impact bench with two plates and a dummy sector inertia: measure rebound and noise at the pinch, with polymer and bare tips. If rebound stays below ±0.2 column without buffers, C becomes the simplest machine of the three despite its mass.

Conclusion¶

The research changes the question. Clean-sheet effort should go first to element inertia and only then to the joints, because at 18 mm the cup element alone contributes about three quarters of candidate A's worst-key mass, and the bearings' contribution to keytop load has no measured value at all. Build the rolling-bar test first: it answers in a day whether the 1880 idea survives laser-cut edges and shallow flanks, and a failure there sends the design straight to B.

The owner has three decisions to make. The first is the stroke, since "15 to 20 mm" and "short" cannot both hold with a key-driven element. The second is whether a cup with a fixed interior backing is acceptable under R-003 and the strike path. The third is the one route to a real short stroke that does not touch stored energy: a 14-column sector with six rows and a bank shift, in the manner of Moya and Schuckers's short segment, cuts the worst-key excursion to ±83.6° and the element term to about 23% (22 g instead of 97 g at 18 mm), or allows a stroke of about 9 to 10 mm at the current mass. It conflicts with the recorded 28-by-3 layout and needs a taller element and a second axis, so it needs a D-record before it can be drawn; I have not proposed it as a candidate.
