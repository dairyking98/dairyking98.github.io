Selection candidates, animated¶

The three mechanisms from the research report, so you can see them move. Click a key to play a stroke, or drag the slider to hold it at any depth.

Every number here is a first estimate, not CAD or test. The rolling bar's slot is computed from its motion, so the slot you see is the one that key would need. The code tree is a schematic. The opposed plates are drawn to scale in plan, with the two plates pulled apart so both are visible.

Rank 1: one rolling bar, a slot in every lever¶

Side view, to scale, of one lever. The tail rises and takes the bar into its slot. The slot's lean carries the bar forward or back on its hanger, and the straight end of the slot holds it still while the lock goes in. Try t (shallowest slot) and w (steepest). Left-hand keys lean one way and right-hand keys the other, and both drive the same bar, so there is no idle side.

Two things the drawing exposed that the report doesn't settle:

  • One radius only fits one row. The report puts the slot 45 mm behind every lever's pivot, but your three keytop rows have pivots 19 mm apart and one bar sits at one place. Either all 28 levers share one pivot rod with different front lengths, or each row gets its own slot radius (about 60, 79 and 98 mm on the current layout) and a longer tail rise. This view shows the 45 mm case.
  • The element is drawn end-on here, and that may not be how it mounts. A sector on a horizontal rock shaft meshes with a pinion on a horizontal axis. Your current design has the element on an upright shaft, so a real layout needs a right-angle step, such as a crank and link or a bevel pair.

Rank 2: five code bails summed by a lever tree¶

Schematic. Each key's lugs push some of the five bails, shown as filled chips. Four floating levers add the bails, weighted 1, 2, 4, 8 and minus 16, into one output, so a key's code directly gives the columns to move. The rest position is a printing column, and the longest move is 14 columns. Every lever in the tree is free to float, and nothing slides in the summing path.

Rank 3: Richardson's opposed plates¶

Plan view, rear at the top, based on US554730. Two plates are linked so one goes back as the other comes forward. A key tip pushes whichever plate is nearer until the other arrives, and the two pinch the tip. Each key's pair of notches sets where that happens. There is no home position, so press a few keys in a row and watch it go straight from one letter to the next.
